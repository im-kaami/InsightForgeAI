Lesson 0001 · InsightForge roadmap

From "ask a question, get a chart" to a full data science app

A picture-first explanation of what InsightForge does today, what is missing, and the six phases that would turn it into a complete data science application.

About 15 minutes. Written on 2026-09-27 from the code in this repository and the research linked in Sources. Hover over any dotted word to see what it means.

Your mission: turn InsightForge from a tool that writes SQL and draws charts into a full data science application: one that can test ideas, forecast, predict and warn you. It must keep the trust features you already built.
One picture to keep in your head: think of InsightForge as a junior analyst you hired. Today the analyst is honest and careful about where data comes from, but answers every question with one quick query and never double-checks. Each phase of the roadmap trains that analyst, step by step, into a senior data scientist.
Short on time? Look at the diagrams and read the colored "After this phase" boxes. That is 80% of the lesson.

1. Where InsightForge is today

When you ask a question, six things happen in a straight line. An AI model writes a plan, a safety check approves the SQL, DuckDB runs it, and you get a chart and a written summary.

The five weak spots, in plain words

  1. It plans once and never looks back. The AI writes up to 6 steps at the start and runs them blindly, like a vending machine. If step 2 gives a strange result, nobody notices.
  2. "Local only" mode is not really useful. It is the default privacy setting, but it always swaps in a basic stand-in AI, even if you run a real AI on your own computer. The result is only simple counts.
  3. Results can be cut off silently. Queries are capped at 10,000 rows, and the screen then reports 10,000 as the total, with no warning.
  4. Charts only draw the first 500 rows for bar, line and pie charts, again with no warning.
  5. Summary numbers are not linked to evidence. Your checked sales report links every number to its source; ordinary chat answers do not.

The good news: your trust layer is rare. A survey of 45 AI data science agents found more than 90% had no explicit trust or safety features.[3] In another study, 74% of business users admitted they had shipped a wrong number that came from AI.[8] People want answers they can check, and that is exactly what you already care about.

Report card: the data science lifecycle

Data science work usually moves through these stages. Here is how InsightForge scores on each.

2. The two big ideas behind the whole plan

Big idea 1: from vending machine to analyst loop

A good human analyst does not plan everything upfront. They run a query, look at the result, notice something odd, adjust, and double-check before answering. Research agents that work this way score much better. Google's DS-STAR repeats plan, code and check for up to 10 rounds, and raised its score on the hard DABStep benchmark from 41.0% to 45.2%.[1] Data Interpreter, which checks each step, went from 75.9% to 94.9% on another benchmark.[2]

Big idea 2: the AI chooses, tested code calculates

AI models are good at understanding questions and poor at arithmetic and statistics. A 2026 study found that AI agents make subtle mistakes in statistical tests even when their code runs without errors.[6] So the AI should only pick the method and the columns. Well-tested code, like a statistics library, does the actual maths. Your checked sales report already works this way; the plan extends the idea to everything.

3. The roadmap at a glance

Phase 0 · Fix the leaks

Make what exists honest and useful

Analyst analogy: before teaching new skills, fix the habits that can mislead you today, like reading only part of a report without saying so.
Before
The table says Total rows: 10,000. The real answer had 2.3 million rows, but the query was quietly capped.
After
The table says Showing 10,000 of 2,341,556 rows, and charts summarize all the data instead of the first 500 rows.
Before
"Local only" mode uses a stand-in AI, so private data only gets basic counts.
After
"Local only" can use an AI running on your computer, so private data gets real analysis without leaving your machine.
Before
The data health check only counts rows, blanks and duplicates.
After
It also shows value ranges, outliers, numbers stored as text and unusual columns, using a well-known checklist.[24]

Also in this phase

  • Faster runs: reuse the saved table layout instead of rescanning all data every time.
  • More chart types (box plots, heatmaps, maps) and switching chart type without asking the AI again.
  • Suggested follow-up questions, and a simple SQL editor for people who know SQL.
  • Cost tracking per AI provider, and a proper job queue so long analyses survive restarts.
After this phase you can: trust that what you see is complete, and use private mode for real work.

Why first: these are mostly small changes, they fix correctness problems, and every later phase depends on honest results.

Technical names (for talking to developers) Detect the query limit by fetching limit + 1 rows; downsample in SQL before plotting; mark self-hosted providers as "local-safe" in privacy.py; use DuckDB SUMMARIZE for profiling; cache schema per dataset version; request strict JSON-schema output from the model; add a worker queue (Celery, RQ or similar) and Docker Compose.
Phase 1 · Analyst loop

Teach it to think in steps and double-check

Analyst analogy: the junior analyst learns to take one step, look at the result, adjust, and double-check before handing you an answer. When your question is vague, they ask.

What gets built

  • The loop itself, with limits on steps, time and cost, and a Quick or Deep setting (products like ThoughtSpot offer the same choice[17]).
  • Clarifying questions: "Last month or last quarter?" shown as buttons you can click.
  • A privacy filter that decides what the AI may see after each step (see Safety rails).
  • Self-checks: did a join accidentally double the rows? Every number in the summary is linked to the table cell it came from, plus an Assumptions box ("I used order_date and ignored blank amounts").
  • Real memory of findings instead of a 300-character note.
  • An exam for the AI: 30 to 50 questions with known answers, rerun after every change, so quality never drops without anyone noticing. Developers call this an evaluation harness.
After this phase you can: ask vague or multi-part questions and get a checked answer that shows its assumptions.

Why now: every later feature becomes a tool plugged into this loop. Building the exam at the same time protects quality from here on.

Technical names Plan-act-observe-verify controller; tool registry with typed Pydantic inputs and outputs; ask_user tool; per-mode observation policy; verifier with row-count and join fan-out checks plus an LLM judge; claim-to-evidence mapping reused from verified_report.py; structured findings memory; step tracing.
Phase 2 · Science toolbox

Give it a scientific calculator and a locked lab

Why the robustness check matters

A 2026 study gave the same data to many AI "analysts". Their conclusions varied widely and often flipped between runs.[7] Re-running a key conclusion with reasonable alternatives, and saying whether it holds, is a cheap defense.

The locked room (sandbox)

Some questions need custom code. That code runs in a sealed room: no internet, time and memory limits, read-only access to your data. Results from custom code are labeled differently from the tested methods. Services such as E2B provide this kind of room.[18] One warning from the research: a popular shortcut (running Python inside Pyodide) is not safe enough, and its authors shut down their project for that reason.[19]

A notebook view also arrives here: every step the AI took becomes an editable, rerunnable cell, like Julius and Hex offer.[21][20]

After this phase you can: ask "is this real?", "why did it change?" and "what happens next?", and get answers backed by proven methods.

Why here: it needs the loop from Phase 1, and it is the fastest way to go "beyond SQL" while keeping results repeatable.

Technical names New step and artifact types for: Welch t-test, Mann-Whitney, ANOVA, chi-square, correlation with confidence intervals, effect sizes, multiple-comparison correction (scipy, statsmodels, pingouin); regression with diagnostics; period-over-period contribution analysis; IQR and Isolation Forest anomalies; k-means or HDBSCAN; CUPED and sequential testing; forecasting with statsforecast baselines and Chronos-Bolt or TimesFM; pluggable sandbox (Docker for development, gVisor or self-hosted E2B for production).
Phase 3 · Clean data + models

Clean the kitchen, then cook

Analyst analogy: good predictions need clean ingredients. The analyst learns to clean data the same way every time, then to build prediction models, and to keep an eye on them after they go live.

What gets built

  • Cleaning recipes: fix types, parse dates, remove duplicates, join tables, add calculated columns. Each run creates a new version you review, so nothing changes behind your back.
  • Validation rules checked on every upload: "order ID is never blank", "price is above zero".
  • "Predict this column" wizard: the app tries many models and shows a ranked leaderboard. This is called AutoML. A newer model called TabPFN beat four hours of tuned models in about three seconds on small tables (up to 10,000 rows).[11]
  • Explanations: which columns drive the predictions, using a method called SHAP.
  • Monitoring: alert when new data stops looking like the data the model learned from. This is called drift; tools like Evidently detect it.[14]

AI agents are getting good at this work: the best agents on the MLE-bench benchmark now earn a medal in about 64% of Kaggle competitions, up from 16.9% when it launched.[13]

After this phase you can: predict things like "which customers will stop buying" and know when the prediction stops being reliable.

Why here: models need clean data and the sandbox from Phase 2. Linking models to data versions keeps your trust promise.

Technical names Recipe engine compiled to DuckDB SQL producing draft versions; validation contracts (Pandera or Great Expectations style); join-key discovery; FLAML, AutoGluon, TabPFN (check its license); leakage detection; ROC, precision-recall, calibration; SHAP and permutation importance; model registry tied to dataset version IDs; batch scoring and a predict endpoint; Evidently drift reports.
Phase 4 · Business dictionary

One meaning for every business word, then automatic alerts

Analyst analogy: the analyst gets the company dictionary, so "revenue" means the same thing to everyone. Then they watch your key numbers every morning and warn you when something looks wrong.

Why it matters so much: on a realistic business SQL benchmark, the best agent in the original study solved only 21% of tasks.[4] A 2026 paper reports 94% on a version of the same benchmark by letting the AI ask the metric dictionary instead of writing raw SQL.[5] (Different setups and years, so treat it as a strong direction, not a head-to-head score.) Analysts agree: 65% say AI works best when business logic is defined centrally.[9]

Then: numbers that watch themselves

You follow "weekly revenue"→ Checked every morning→ Unusual drop found→ Driver analysis: "North region, product X"→ Slack or email alert with the reason

This is how Tableau Pulse works.[16] Also in this phase: a library of approved question-and-SQL examples the AI learns from (Snowflake's approach[15]), turning your single sales report into checked reports for any metric, and an "approved data only" mode for business users (Hex's approach[20]).

After this phase you can: trust that "revenue" means one thing everywhere, and hear about problems before you go looking.

Why here: alerts reuse the Phase 2 toolbox on a schedule. If your users are business people rather than data scientists, you could build this before Phase 3.

Technical names YAML semantic model (entities, dimensions, measures, metrics, relationships, synonyms) compatible with Apache Ossie (formerly Open Semantic Interchange) and dbt; metric queries compiled deterministically to SQL; verified query repository; endorsements; local value index for filter matching; metric following with threshold, anomaly and trend-change checks; digest delivery.
Phase 5 · Open the doors

Let teams in, and plug into the tools people already use

Analyst analogy: the analyst joins the team: shares work, takes comments, gets approvals, and answers questions wherever people already are, such as Slack or an AI assistant.
  • Teams: organizations, roles (admin, editor, viewer), sign-in with your company account, audit logs.
  • Sharing: dashboards, read-only links, comments on results, approval steps for metrics and models.
  • More data: big company databases, cloud storage, and business apps; analysis of text columns and maps.
After this phase you can: run InsightForge as a team, and ask it questions from the tools you already use every day.

Why last: sharing is most valuable once there are trustworthy metrics, models and alerts worth sharing.

Technical names Organizations and RBAC, OIDC or SAML single sign-on, audit logs, retention cleanup; dashboards and parameterized apps; MCP server and client; REST API and Python SDK; Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks and SQL Server connectors; S3, GCS and ADLS with Parquet, Iceberg and Delta; SaaS ingestion via dlt or Airbyte; AI SQL functions gated by privacy mode; DuckDB spatial, fts and vss extensions.

Most trust at the top. Only 3% of analysts in a 2026 survey wanted fully self-driving AI; most wanted to stay in the loop.[9] Top trust requests from data leaders were audit trails and repeatable results.[10]

5. The one decision only you can make

Who is the main user? The answer changes the order of Phases 3 and 4.

  • Business people (owners, operations, finance): do Phase 4 (dictionary and alerts) before Phase 3 (models).
  • Data scientists: keep the order; notebooks, the sandbox and models matter most.

Also worth knowing: your project guide (AGENTS.md) currently focuses on the Verified Spreadsheet Report for small-business teams and defers dashboards. This roadmap is the broader "full data science" path you asked about. They fit together: Phases 0, 1 and 4 directly strengthen the report product, while Phases 2 and 3 add the science.

6. Check your understanding

Answer from memory first; recalling is what makes it stick. You get feedback instantly.

1. Why does InsightForge need a loop instead of one plan?
A loop lets the AI look at each result, notice problems and adjust, like a human analyst. That is what raised scores for DS-STAR and Data Interpreter (Big idea 1).
2. Under "the AI chooses, tested code calculates", who computes the numbers?
The AI only picks the method and columns. Proven code does the maths, and the summary may only quote numbers from a result card (Big idea 2).
3. What problem does the metric dictionary (semantic layer) solve?
One written definition, used by chat, reports and alerts alike (Phase 4, Figure 8).
4. In schema-only privacy mode, what can the AI see after a step?
It sees the shape of the result (names, types, row counts, errors) so it can still correct itself, but never the values (Safety rail 1).
5. Why does Phase 0 ("fix the leaks") come first?
Queries are capped at 10,000 rows and some charts at 500, without warning. Everything later depends on honest, complete results.
6. In Phase 3, what does "drift" mean?
When the world changes, a model trained on old data gets less accurate. Watching for drift tells you when to retrain (Figure 7, step 8).
7. Which kind of result deserves the most trust?
Certified metrics sit at the top of the trust ladder: an approved definition plus automatic checks (Safety rail 2).

Glossary

A/B test
Comparing two versions of something (like two prices) to see which performs better, using statistics to rule out luck.
Agent
An AI that can take actions (run queries, draw charts) in several steps, not just write text.
AI model (LLM)
The text AI, such as GPT or Llama, that reads your question and writes plans and SQL.
Anomaly detection
Automatically finding values far outside the normal pattern.
AutoML
Software that tries many prediction models for you and ranks them by accuracy.
Deterministic
Always gives the same output for the same input. Tested code is deterministic; AI models are not.
Drift
New data slowly stops looking like the data a model learned from, so predictions get worse.
DuckDB
The fast analytics database inside InsightForge that stores your tables and runs SQL.
Evaluation harness
A fixed exam of questions with known answers, rerun after every change to catch quality drops.
Evidence
A link from a number in a summary back to the exact table cell it came from.
Forecasting
Predicting future values over time, with a range showing uncertainty.
Hypothesis test
A standard method for deciding whether a difference is likely real or just chance.
Local model
An AI that runs on your own computer (for example with Ollama), so data never leaves it.
MCP
Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants like Claude use outside tools.
Metric dictionary (semantic layer)
Written business definitions, such as what "revenue" means, used by every query and chart.
Privacy mode
InsightForge's per-dataset setting for what may be sent to an AI: local, schema only, or full.
Safety check (SQL guard)
Code that inspects every query and blocks anything that is not read-only.
Sandbox
A sealed environment where code runs without internet and with strict limits.
Segmentation
Splitting customers or items into groups that behave alike (also called clustering).
SHAP
A method that shows how much each column pushed a prediction up or down.
Tool
One ability the AI can call, like "run SQL". The AI chooses the tool; the tool's code does the work.
Version (dataset version)
A frozen, fingerprinted copy of your data at one point in time, so results can be reproduced.
